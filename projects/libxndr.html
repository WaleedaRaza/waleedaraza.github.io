THE WRITE-UP

My dad gave me a Kindle Scribe for my nineteenth birthday. Discovering I could import PDFs sent me down a rabbit hole.

I built a tool that searched for books and authors, opened candidate PDFs, and helped me assemble a collection. The first version involved a lot of browser tabs, misleading results, manual cleanup, and a terminal script that saved files and closed tabs. It wasn't elegant, but it did something I wanted.

Then a friend I frequently debate with told me I only read subjective books. I disagreed, and because he wasn't buying a Kindle, I made a site where he could explore the collection himself.

He barely read my recommendations. But he asked a better question: wasn't the value in the ability to source books, rather than only in the books I'd happened to curate?

The question that changed the product

That shifted Libxndr from a display of my collection toward a tool someone else could use for their own interests.

I added the sourcing workflow to the site. Users can find books, open PDF links, and choose to contribute discoveries to the shared library. It now contains more than 450 books. I've been improving the interface and making the application easier to approach without an explanation from me.

I'm not trying to claim a large audience. I'd like a handful of power users who find it useful enough to keep using and help shape it.

What I'm exploring next

I'm working on ways to ask a question and be directed to specific sections of a book through a parsed structure of the text.

The distinction matters to me: an answer about a book isn't the same experience as finding the passage, reading what surrounds it, and deciding whether the answer holds up. I want AI to help someone reach the source, not become the only thing they read.

Libxndr started as a way to get books onto my Kindle. Its more interesting direction came from someone questioning what I'd actually built.
